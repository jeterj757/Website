Wampold et al. — Comparing bona fide psychotherapies

Psychological Bulletin · 1997 · meta-analysis
open as tab
Magnitude versus variability

Magnitude asks whether the average difference between bona fide treatments is meaningfully different from zero. Variability asks whether the observed treatment differences scatter around zero more than sampling error would predict.

The questions are related but not interchangeable: a near-zero mean can hide offsetting differences, so the homogeneity test matters.

Interpretive guardrail

Little evidence of average differences among bona fide therapies does not mean psychotherapy is ineffective, nor that every intervention works equally well for every problem or person.

Chapter 8 — Existential Psychotherapy

Yalom & Josselson · pp. 273–305 · due Sep 8
open as tab
Main idea

Existential therapy is less a freestanding technical school than a way of understanding the human situation. It focuses on the anxieties that follow from awareness of existence and can be integrated with other approaches.

main ideaexistential givens
Four ultimate concerns
DeathFinitude and the wish to continue being.
FreedomResponsibility for authorship, choice, and the absence of fixed ground.
IsolationThe unbridgeable separation that remains even within close relationships.
MeaninglessnessThe task of creating purpose in a world without an automatically given design.
View of distress

Symptoms may function as defenses against existential anxiety. People narrow possibility through responsibility avoidance, compulsive patterns, dependence, or special beliefs that protect them from vulnerability and finitude.

Therapist stance and process

The therapist works as a fellow traveler: present, genuine, and attentive to the here-and-now relationship. The work helps clients recognize choice, assume responsibility, engage others more fully, and confront rather than mute the givens of existence.

Mechanism of change

Awareness plus responsibility expands choice. Change comes through examining how the client constructs a life, experimenting with decisions, deepening encounter, and using mortality awareness as an awakening rather than only a threat.

Evidence and limitation

The chapter presents existential principles as widely influential but difficult to manualize as a discrete package. That flexibility supports integration, while also making theory-specific outcome testing and mechanism attribution harder.

Exam-ready distinction

Existential anxiety is an unavoidable consequence of awareness; it is not automatically pathology. Therapy asks whether a person uses that anxiety to live more deliberately or organizes life around avoiding it.

Articles · Existential unit

Frankl — The Will to Meaning

journal article · pp. 82–88 · due Sep 8
open as tab
Central claim

Frankl argues that the distinctively human motivational struggle is a will to meaning, not merely a will to pleasure or power. Patients may bring philosophical and spiritual problems to therapy even when those problems are not symptoms of disease.

Diagnostic distinctions
Existential frustrationAn unfulfilled search for meaning; not inherently pathological or necessarily pathogenic.
Noogenic neurosisA neurosis that develops when conflict or frustration in the meaning/spiritual dimension becomes pathogenic.
Psychogenic / somatogenicPsychological or bodily origins that must not be collapsed into a meaning-based explanation.
Two errors Frankl rejects

Pathologism/psychologism: reducing genuine human questions to symptoms or complexes. Spiritualism: treating every neurosis as noogenic. Frankl cites clinic samples reporting only about 12% as noogenic to argue for discrimination rather than a total explanation.

Therapist role

Logotherapy calls forth the client’s capacity to orient toward meaning and clarifies values or tasks that can be realized. The therapist does not simply explain away despair; the person’s question is taken seriously on its own level.

Mechanism of change

Survival and endurance become possible when the person experiences a concrete “why”: a person, work, task, responsibility, or transcendent witness that expects something from them.

Clinical caution

The historical article discusses despair and suicide in language that differs from contemporary risk practice. Its claim that despair need not itself be pathological should not be read as minimizing the need for direct suicide-risk assessment and safety intervention.

Connection to Chapter 8

Frankl supplies a focused theory of meaning; Chapter 8 places meaninglessness beside death, freedom, and isolation within a broader existential formulation.

Yalom — The “Terrestrial” Meanings of Life

International Forum for Logotherapy · 1982 · pp. 92–102 · due Sep 8
open as tab
Cosmic and terrestrial meaning

Cosmic meaning asks whether life belongs to an overarching, externally given order. Terrestrial meaning asks what gives one particular life purpose and coherence; it can be entirely secular.

Sources of terrestrial meaning
altruismdedication to a causecreativityliving fullyself-actualizationself-transcendence

These sources can overlap; Yalom treats them as pathways rather than a single prescription.

Clinical illustrations

Cases of people facing terminal cancer show how service, generativity, modeling, and influence on others can preserve purpose in the presence of suffering and death. The clinical emphasis is not denial of mortality but meaningful participation despite it.

Self-transcendence

Yalom gives special weight to moving beyond self-preoccupation toward a person, cause, creation, or future generation. He uses Frankl to show why happiness and self-actualization may emerge indirectly when they are not pursued as isolated ends.

Developmental nuance

Meaning sources can change over the life cycle toward generativity. Yalom also notes a gendered complication: altruism imposed on women is different from freely chosen self-transcendence, so the same behavior may not carry the same meaning.

Therapist application

Explore both what clients seek from relationships and what they contribute. Ask where their daily activity expresses service, creativity, commitment, full engagement, or growth—and whether the source of meaning is chosen or inherited.

Connection to Frankl

Frankl argues that meaning is a primary human orientation; Yalom makes the idea clinically concrete by cataloging secular routes through which a life can become purposeful.

Reusable workflow

Study tips & notation

A lightweight first pass before the full dossier, followed by active recall and a two-page seminar sheet.

Three-phase reading workflow

  1. While reading: mark only what is new or important with the margin codes below.
  2. After each chapter: spend five handwritten minutes on 2–5 sentences: the chapter’s argument and its connection to the book’s thesis.
  3. After the book: type the summaries, add page numbers, collect coded notes, check criticisms for fairness, then compress the formal notes to two pages.

Fairness rule: judge a reading against what it set out to do, not against a different project you wish it had attempted.

Retention that earns its time

  • Use spaced repetition instead of cramming.
  • Use active recall: explain from memory, then correct what you missed.
  • Try the Feynman technique and teach the concept in plain language.
  • Cluster one narrow topic from memory on a whiteboard.
  • Attach abstract concepts to lived examples for elaborative encoding.
  • Rotate which subject begins and ends a session.
  • Work in 45–60 minute blocks and protect sleep.

Fast article triage

  1. Read the abstract.
  2. If the hypothesis is unclear, skim the first and last introduction paragraphs.
  3. Read the conclusion for the results.
  4. Return to methods only for definitions, sample, or procedure details.

Exam, writing & course habits

  • Practice tests and self-written professor-style questions are high-yield.
  • Outline papers by section and place citations into each block while drafting.
  • Time-block study sessions and visit office hours early.
  • Learn which courses are driven by textbooks versus lecture slides.
  • Convert slides to annotatable PDFs; print them before lecture when useful.
